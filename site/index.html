What is computed

For every hour from 2026 to 2030, LunaLens takes the points on the Moon directly beneath the Sun and Earth from JPL Horizons and converts them to azimuth and elevation at each site. A site's skyline is the highest terrain in each direction, sampled every 0.5° out to 460 km over LOLA elevation data, seen from 2 m above the ground on a curved Moon. The Sun counts as in view when its upper limb clears the skyline, and Earth when its centre does. Positions between hourly samples are interpolated, and rise and set times are refined by bisection.

Data

  • Sun and Earth positions: NASA/JPL Horizons, DE441, fetched 13 November 2026. Moon mean-Earth/polar-axis frame, corrected for the Moon's rotation during light travel time.
  • Terrain: LRO LOLA gridded elevation, LDEM_75S_240M (240 m) and LDEM_85S_40M (40 m), NASA Planetary Data System.
  • Use in calculations: custom coordinates use the 240 m grid. Polar presets combine 40 m near-field terrain with the 240 m grid farther out. The 480 m grid is a downsampled display layer and is not used for visibility calculations.
  • Map relief: hillshade rendered by the team from LDEM_75S_240M.
  • Site coordinates: listed below with their sources.

Checks we ran

  • The browser engine and a Python implementation give identical hourly sunlight, Earth-visibility and simultaneous-visibility totals for all twelve preset sites over 14 November–14 December 2026 and calendar year 2027.
  • A direct 240 m skyline recomputation at IM-1 differed from its stored profile by at most 0.005° on the current data. The check's pass threshold is 0.02°. Finer-resolution presets use additional terrain and are expected to differ.
  • These are consistency checks, not a measure of absolute terrain accuracy or landing-site certification. The repository includes an independent Horizons fixture check; an independent terrain ray-tracing comparison has not yet been added.

Limits

  • Ridge-top results depend on terrain within a few hundred metres, which these grids cannot resolve. Terrain closer than two grid cells (80 m or 480 m) is ignored. Changing this cut-off can substantially change the predicted sunlight.
  • Coordinates you add use 240 m terrain only. Their results can differ substantially from the finer-resolution presets. Treat them as a first look, especially near a ridge or crater edge.
  • Sites north of 75°S have no terrain model here, so their horizon is a smooth sphere.
  • The polar rim sites are locations computed by LunaLens, the highest point of each rim crest in the 240 m grid. They are not official landing sites.
  • Totals use hourly samples; short events between samples can be missed. Countdown times are interpolated estimates.
  • Visibility is yes or no. Partial solar power, panel orientation, eclipses, thermal limits, radio link budgets and relay satellites are not modelled.

Computation

Everything on this page is computed in your browser from the data files, so it works with no server. The rules for sunlight, link and window totals follow the backend in backend/lunalens/windows.

Validation status

Our share of time in sunlight has been compared with NASA's own long-term map. At 120 points sampled across the south polar cap (80°S–90°S), the skyline from our 240 m grid and the full 2026–2030 ephemeris is compared with PGDA product 69 (Mazarico et al. 2011, 18.6 years):

  • Spearman rank correlation 0.98, Pearson 0.985.
  • Mean difference +1.3 percentage points, RMS 2.7 points.
  • Worst point +15.1 points at 82.7°S 137.3°E, on ground where both maps are low and a grid cell decides the answer.

What the comparison does not cover: we average five years where NASA averages 18.6, we sample every tenth hour, we count the Sun as up when any part of its disc clears the skyline while NASA's product does not state its rule, and our grid is 240 m against their 120 m. The script is site/tools/check_pgda.mjs.

Atlas page sources

The Atlas page is a separate application with its own data and its own credits; it does not reuse the files behind Mission or Explore. Its sources:

  • Average sunlight, average Earth view and permanent shadow: NASA GSFC PGDA product 69, 2016-08 release, 120 m, 75°S–90°S. Mazarico et al. (2011), Icarus 211, 1066–1081. Averages over 18.6 years, one lunar nodal cycle.
  • Elevation and slope: LOLA south-polar tiles, 296.9 m and 80 m per pixel, derived from the LRO LOLA gridded products.
  • Sun and Earth positions: JPL DE440 through SPICE, hourly 2024–2036, with the observer's position on the surface applied in the browser.
  • Landing sites: 41 past, failed and planned missions. Coordinates come with that application and have not been re-checked against primary sources here.

Where the two pages disagree on a number, the reason is usually the sunlight rule: this page counts the Sun as up when any part of its disc clears the terrain, and the Atlas page defaults to "at least half the disc" for its cards.

Glossary

Azimuth
Angle clockwise from north: north 0°, east 90°.
Elevation
Angle above the local horizon. Negative means below it.
Lunar day
Mean interval between successive passages of the Sun across the same lunar longitude: about 29.53 Earth days.
Libration
The slow rocking of the Moon that moves Earth in the lunar sky.
Direct-to-Earth link
Earth’s centre is above the terrain skyline plus the link margin.
Sun disk fraction
The part of the Sun’s disk above the skyline. The Sun counts as up when any part is visible.
